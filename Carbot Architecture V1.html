01

The full pipeline

The track asks the car for nine different things: stop at a gate, change lane around an obstacle, pick an exit off the roundabout, get through a dark tunnel, over a bump, up a hill, obey a light, then park twice in two different ways.

The tempting move is to write nine pieces of code, one per challenge. Don't. They all end up fighting over the same steering servo, and the moment two of them are true at once — you're in the roundabout and the gate is down — you have no idea which one wins.

Instead there's one pipeline, running every frame, always in the same order. The nine challenges stop being nine programs and become nine different sets of numbers flowing through the same blocks.

The three layers

Read the diagram top to bottom and each layer is answering a question for the one below it.

  • Perception answers what is around me? It builds a picture and decides nothing at all.
  • Planning answers where do I want the car to be? It turns the picture into a decision, and the decision into a curve.
  • Control answers what do I do with the wheel and the throttle right now? It has no opinion about the curve it was handed.

That split is worth defending, because it's what stops the code turning into a knot. When the car misbehaves you get to ask one question first — did it see the world wrong, or did it see it right and decide badly? — and the answer sends you to one layer instead of all of it.

The two sensor chains

Look at the top of the diagram and there are two groups going down two separate paths. The cameras answer where am I in the lane, to within a couple of centimetres. The pose sensors answer where am I on the track, to within about twenty.

They meet in exactly one place, the local map, and nowhere else. Keeping them apart until then is deliberate: the moment a coarse position estimate gets anywhere near your steering, the car starts wandering into the tape. More on that in 05.

The three coordinate frames

Most bugs in a system like this are frame bugs — the right number in the wrong coordinates. There are only three, and every value in the pipeline lives in exactly one of them.

  • Image frame. Pixels, row and column. Exists only inside 02 and the raw-frame half of 04. Nothing else is allowed to see it.
  • Car frame. Centimetres, origin at the centre of the rear axle, x forward, y left. This is where corridors, landmarks, trajectories and steering all live.
  • Venue frame. Centimetres, origin at a UWB anchor. Only the prior map and 05 speak it.

The local map is where the venue frame gets converted into the car frame and then forgotten about. Below 06, nothing in this pipeline knows the venue frame exists.

The interface rule

A block may only talk to its neighbours, and only through an interface you've written down. No block reaches past the one next to it, and no block cares how its neighbour got the answer.

This sounds like bureaucracy. It's the opposite — it's what lets you build a deliberately stupid version of any block, get the car moving, and replace it later without touching anything around it. You'll do exactly that twice in 14.

Each block below opens with its job, where its input comes from and where its output goes, then the logic that runs inside it.

Perception

Five blocks whose only job is to build a picture of the floor. None of them decides anything.

02

Capture and warp

jobTurn three camera feeds into one flat picture of the floor
inthree raw colour frames — from the camerasone depth frame — from the front RGB-D camera
outground image in cm + floor mask → 03 corridorsraw front frame, depth, non-floor clusters → 04 landmarks

A camera sees the world in perspective. The road narrows as it runs away from you, and the same 30 cm lane is two hundred pixels wide at the bumper and forty at the far end. Every block after this one wants to think in centimetres on the floor, not in pixels that mean a different distance depending on where they sit in the frame.

So this block's whole purpose is to undo the perspective once, properly, at the very front — and hand everything downstream one honest picture. Get it right and the rest of the document gets easier. Get it wrong and every block after it inherits the error.

Camera height and tilt

Your camera sits 8.5 cm off the floor pointing dead level. So it barely sees the road right in front of the car, and most of what it does see is the room. The room is where all the stray white things live — shoes, walls, shiny floor tiles.

You're allowed 25 cm. Put the camera at about 20 and tilt it down 25 to 35 degrees. The road fills the picture, the room drops out of it, and the warp below has real road to work with instead of a sliver. It costs nothing.

Lock exposure and white balance

Left on automatic, the camera quietly readjusts itself every frame. Drive toward a window and your white tape turns grey. Set it by hand once at the venue and leave it alone. With three cameras, set each one separately — they point different ways and see very different light, especially at the mouth of the tunnel.

The warp itself

A homography is a 3×3 matrix that maps one flat plane onto another. The floor is flat, so a single matrix per camera converts image pixels into floor centimetres exactly — no approximation, as long as nothing you care about is sticking up off the floor.

You find it from four correspondences. Tape a grid on the floor, park the car on a marked spot, and for each camera pick four points whose position in centimetres from the rear axle you actually measured. cv2.getPerspectiveTransform solves for the matrix; cv2.warpPerspective applies it.

canvas   : 240 x 200 px, 1 px = 0.5 cm
origin   : rear axle at (120, 190), x forward = up the canvas
coverage : 0 to 100 cm ahead, 60 cm either side

for cam in (front, left, right):
    img = undistort(grab(cam), K[cam], dist[cam])
    warpPerspective(img, H[cam], canvas, dst = ground)

Undistort first, with intrinsics from an ordinary chessboard calibration. A wide-angle lens bends straight lines, and the homography assumes they're straight — skip this and your tape comes out curved at the edges of the frame.

Combining the three views

Each camera gets its own matrix, but all three write into the same canvas. Where two overlap, don't average — averaging two slightly miscalibrated views blurs the tape edge, which is the one thing you need sharp. Pick a winner per region: the front camera owns the middle strip, each side camera owns its own side, and the seam sits where coverage is weakest for both.

This is the reason the block exists as its own box. Everything after it reads one picture and never finds out there were three cameras — so adding a fourth, or losing one to a loose cable, changes nothing above this line.

The ground-plane mask

You have a depth camera and you aren't using it. It solves the stray-white-object problem on its own, and it hands 03 and 04 a large part of their work for free.

The camera is bolted down at a known height h and pitch θ. For any pixel you can compute how far away it would be if it were the floor, purely from geometry — the ray leaves the lens at a known angle, and you solve for where it meets a plane at height zero. Compare that with the depth the sensor actually reported.

for each pixel (u,v):
    ray      = K⁻¹ · [u, v, 1]         # direction in camera frame
    ray_w    = R(θ) · ray              # rotate to a level frame
    expected = h / (-ray_w.z)          # range to the floor plane
    residual = expected - measured

    residual < 2 cm   -> floor
    residual > 2 cm   -> something standing on the floor
    no return         -> unknown, treat as not-floor

Two products come out of this and they go to different places. The floor mask goes to 03, which only ever looks at pixels the mask approves. The rejected pixels — the things standing up — get clustered and passed to 04 as obstacle candidates.

Keep the tolerance loose, around 2 cm. Cheap depth is noisy, the mat has texture, and a tight threshold starts deleting bits of road.

Mount them rigidly. Both the homography and the ground-plane test assume the camera hasn't moved. A bracket that flexes over the speed bump quietly wrecks both, and it will look exactly like a software bug for a very long time.

03

Corridors

jobSay what shape of floor the car is allowed to drive on
inground image + floor mask — from 02 capture and warproundabout geometry — from 07 prior map, placed using 05's pose
outone or more corridors, each a left and right edge in cm → 06 local map

This is the block that replaces your current line follower, and the shift is in the question it asks. The old code asks: where is the white paint? This one asks: what shape of floor am I allowed to drive on?

A camera sees paint. A driver sees a lane. This block is the step in between, and it's the single biggest change in the architecture.

What "the black road" actually means

Start with the obvious approach and watch it fail, because the failure is what motivates the real one.

The naive version is gray < 60 → road. It breaks immediately, and in both directions. Things that are dark but aren't road: the shadow under the boom gate, the shadow the car casts on itself, dark grout between floor tiles, a black obstacle, the inside of the tunnel. Things that are road but aren't dark: the patch directly under a ceiling light, the mat where it's worn, the whole mat once you drive toward a window.

A single number can't separate those, because the thing that makes a pixel road isn't its brightness. It's continuity. Road is the surface the car is currently standing on, extended outward until something stops it.

So don't classify pixels independently. Grow a region.

Region growing from a seed

The trick is that you already know one piece of road for certain: the patch of floor immediately in front of the bumper. The car is on the road — if it isn't, you have a bigger problem than segmentation. So take a small patch at the bottom of the ground image, a few centimetres wide, and call it the seed.

Then flood outward. A neighbouring pixel joins the region if it looks like the region does locally — within some tolerance of the running mean of the pixels it is connecting to. Nothing is ever compared against a fixed constant.

seed  = ground[bottom strip, centre 10 cm]    # road by definition
flood fill from seed, 4-connected:
    accept neighbour p if
        floor_mask[p]                         # from 02: on the ground plane
        and |I(p) - local_mean| < tol         # same surface as its neighbour
    reject otherwise

road_region = the connected component containing the seed

Now look at what this fixes, one failure at a time.

  • The white tape stops the flood. Tape is far outside the tolerance, so the region simply cannot cross it. The tape isn't something you detect — it's the wall the flood hits. That's also why a five-centimetre gap in the tape doesn't matter much: the flood leaks through the gap and is then stopped by the light tile beyond, so you lose a small bite out of the region rather than a whole edge.
  • Dark things elsewhere are never reached. A black obstacle in the road is dark, but the floor mask already deleted it. A dark shadow on the tile outside the track is dark, but the flood would have to cross the tape to get there, and it can't. Unreachable is as good as rejected.
  • Lighting drift stops mattering. The comparison is always local. If the whole mat brightens by 40 levels as you approach a window, the running mean brightens with it and the region is unaffected. Same reason cv2.adaptiveThreshold works where a global threshold doesn't — you're asking "is this like its neighbour", never "is this dark".
  • Self-shadow is absorbed. The car's own shadow is a gradual darkening, not a step, so the running mean follows it down. A hard threshold would cut the region in half at the shadow line.

What it does not fix: the tunnel, where there is no useful contrast at all, and the roundabout, where the region is correct but tells you nothing about which exit is yours. Both are handled below.

From region to two edges

The region is a blob. Everything downstream wants two ordered lists of points. Clean it up, then slice it.

closed = morphologyEx(road_region, CLOSE, 5x5)   # bridge dash gaps
clean  = largest connected component of closed

for s in 0, 2, 4, ... 100 cm ahead:              # slices in the car frame
    row      = clean at distance s
    interval = the run of road pixels containing the centre
    left[s]  = left end of the interval,  in cm
    right[s] = right end of the interval, in cm
    stop if the interval vanishes or exceeds 60 cm

This looks like the row scan in the old code, and it's worth being precise about why it isn't. The old code scans rows of a perspective image hunting for paint, so a row means a different distance at every point across it, and in a corner a row can miss the lane entirely. Here the slices are in the ground frame, where a slice is a real straight line across the floor at a known distance — and what is being sliced is a region that was grown, not a pair of lines that were searched for. The scan is how you read out an answer you already have, not how you get it.

Dividers and the two-lane case

A dashed centre line is white paint sitting inside the region rather than at its border. That's the whole test.

holes = white pixels inside clean, not touching its boundary
fit a line or low-order curve through the hole centroids
if the fit runs along the region and spans > 20 cm:
    split each slice interval at the fit -> two corridors
else:
    one corridor, the whole road

Same code on a normal stretch and on the lane-change section. Nothing special to write, no state to enter.

Handing out a region rather than a line also pays off two blocks later, where it quietly deletes three special cases from the planner. That's 09.

The roundabout

Here the picture genuinely can't help you. The black ring has four gaps in its edge and nothing marks which one is yours. Region growing works perfectly and gives you the whole ring plus all four exits — a correct answer to a useless question.

So the corridor comes from the surveyed map instead. You measured that ring. You know where exit 2 is. 05 tells you where you are on it. Take the ring-and-exit polyline out of the prior map in venue coordinates, transform it into the car frame with the current pose, and hand that out as the corridor.

T_car←venue = inverse(pose)                  # from 05
corridor    = T_car←venue · prior.roundabout(exit = route.exit)

Its accuracy is the pose's accuracy, roughly ±20 cm — unusable as a lane anywhere else on the track, acceptable here because the ring is wide and there is no alternative. Where the camera can still see an edge, keep the camera's edge and take only the topology from the map.

That is why this deserves its own block. Sometimes the answer comes from the camera, sometimes from the map. Nothing after it should have to care which. What comes out is always the same two lists of points.

Corridor {
  left[]    points in cm, car frame
  right[]   points in cm, car frame
  source    CAMERA | MAP | HELD
  valid     bool
}

Have an answer for "the flood failed". In the tunnel, or if the seed lands on something that isn't road, the region comes back tiny or absurd. Check its area and width before publishing. If it fails, mark the corridor HELD, hand out the last good one shifted by the odometry, and let 08 decide to slow down. Never publish a corridor you don't believe.

04

Landmarks

jobSpot the things the car reacts to rather than drives inside
inraw front frame — from 02, not the warped imagedepth and non-floor clusters — from 02
outtyped detections with a position in cm → 06 local maplight and gate state → 12 safety reflex, directly

Corridors are geometry: continuous, always there, always the same shape of answer. Landmarks are events: a light that is red now, a gate arm that is down now, a sign 40 cm ahead. Different question, different code, different ways of going wrong.

Keeping them apart matters more than it sounds. If the two share a block, a traffic light detector that chokes on a reflection can take your lane finding down with it — and the car stops following the road because it couldn't read a lamp.

Why this block reads the raw frame

Almost everything here is above the floor: the lamp, the gate arm, the sign face, the obstacle. The warp in 02 is a floor-to-floor mapping, so anything off the floor comes out smeared and wrong, and the ground-plane mask deliberately deletes it. The warped image is the right input for corridors and the wrong input for landmarks.

So this block takes the raw frame and the depth map. Detection happens in the image; only the final position is converted to centimetres, using depth for things standing up and the homography for things lying on the floor.

Traffic light

Don't hunt the whole frame for red. You get exit signs, a red jacket in the crowd, and a warm reflection off the mat.

The light is a fixed structure: three lamps stacked vertically in a dark housing. Find that, then read it.

1. restrict to the upper part of the frame; once the pose is
   good, only where the prior map says the light should project
2. find bright saturated blobs; group any that are vertically
   stacked, similar in size, about one diameter apart
3. within the group, decide by RANK, not by colour:
       lit   = the lamp with the highest value channel
       state = TOP -> red, MIDDLE -> amber, BOTTOM -> green
4. sanity: exactly one lamp clearly brighter than the others,
   otherwise return UNKNOWN

Reading it by position rather than by hue is what makes it survive bad white balance, a warm venue, and an overexposed lamp that reads as white rather than red. You are asking which of three lamps is lit, and that question has a correct answer even when the colour is wrong.

Boom gate

Don't look for black-and-white stripes. Look for a bar.

A lowered arm is a long horizontal object crossing the road at roughly 5 to 15 cm above the floor. Depth sees that directly: a band of returns at a consistent short range, spanning the road width, well above the ground plane. Raised, the band is gone.

band  = non-floor pixels whose height above the floor is 5..15 cm
gate  = DOWN if the band spans > 60% of the corridor width
        UP   otherwise
range = median depth of the band -> distance to the stop line

Colour never enters it, which matters because the arm is dark, the tunnel behind it is dark, and the mat below it is dark.

Signs and obstacles

Signs are small, upright, and have a distinctive colour and shape. A hand-written detector — colour mask, contour, aspect ratio, then match the inner glyph against a few templates — is faster to build and far easier to debug than a trained model, and the RDK X5's accelerator isn't worth the pipeline complexity for four sign types. Keep the model option in reserve if the templates prove brittle.

Obstacles need no recognition at all. 02 already handed you every pixel that isn't floor.

for c in clusters of the non-floor mask:
    foot   = the lowest pixels of c            # where it meets the floor
    pos    = H · foot                          # homography -> cm
    extent = width of c at the floor
    keep if pos falls inside the current corridor and extent > 3 cm

You never find out whether it's a cone, a box or a shoe, and you never need to.

Debouncing

Every detection here can flip a state machine, so a single bad frame can stop the car dead. Require agreement across frames before publishing a change, and make the counts asymmetric: three consecutive frames to go from green to red, six to go back. Being slow to start is free; being slow to stop is a penalty.

Reporting positions

A detection that says "red light at row 96, column 212" is nearly useless, because the moment you stop at the line the light is above and behind the camera and the detection is gone. A detection that says "red light, 35 cm ahead, 12 cm to the right" can be held on to.

Detection {
  type    TRAFFIC_LIGHT | GATE | SIGN_PARK | SIGN_BUMP | OBSTACLE
  state   enum, type-specific
  pos     x, y in cm, car frame
  extent  cm, obstacles only
  conf    0..1
  stamp   frame time
}

That's the whole reason landmarks report positions on the floor: so the next block can remember them after they leave the view. Which is the next block's entire job.

05

Localisation

jobWork out where the car is on the track as a whole
inUWB position fixes, gyro rate, wheel ticks — from the sensors
outabsolute pose in venue cm + covariance → 06, 07smooth odometry increment → 06, used every tick

This block answers a completely different question from the cameras, at a completely different scale, and the discipline is in never letting the two swap jobs.

What UWB is for

UWB is off by roughly the width of your whole lane. Steer from it and the car will wander into the tape. So the camera keeps the steering. UWB answers the thing the camera is bad at: which part of the track am I on, and what's coming next?

The filter

Don't try to get position out of the accelerometer. Adding it up twice drifts badly within seconds. Use each sensor for the one thing it's good at and let an EKF hold them together.

state  x = [ px, py, θ, b_gyro ]

predict, every wheel tick (~50 Hz):
    ω   = gyro_z - b_gyro
    v   = (ticks_L + ticks_R) / 2 · k
    px += v·cos(θ)·dt
    py += v·sin(θ)·dt
    θ  += ω·dt
    b_gyro unchanged        # slow random walk, small Q

update, on each UWB fix (~10 Hz):
    z = [ux, uy]
    gate: reject if Mahalanobis distance > 3σ
    standard EKF correction, R ≈ (20 cm)²

Three things worth understanding about why this shape works:

  • The gyro bias is what you're really estimating. A cheap MEMS gyro has an offset that drifts with temperature. Integrate it raw and your heading rotates slowly even when parked. Because the filter also sees position fixes, and position over time implies heading, the bias becomes observable while the car is moving — so it gets estimated and subtracted.
  • The gate is not optional. UWB multipath produces occasional fixes that are a metre out. Without a rejection test, one of those yanks your pose across the track and the mission planner decides you have teleported into the tunnel.
  • Two outputs, two purposes. The corrected pose is absolute and jumps slightly whenever a fix lands. The prediction step alone gives a smooth increment with no jumps at all. 06 uses the smooth one to carry the map forward and the absolute one only to look things up. Never let the jumpy one near geometry that steering will see.

The covariance matters as much as the pose. When it grows past a threshold, 07 should stop trusting "which section am I in" and fall back to what the camera can see.

The tunnel. UWB struggles to get through structure, and it's the same stretch where the camera is weakest. The filter has to get through on the gyro and wheels alone, with covariance growing the whole way. Test that bit early — everything else ends up tuned around it.

Your map is measured from the anchors. If someone nudges an anchor between the survey and the competition, the whole map shifts and nothing warns you. Fix them down properly, and add a startup check: park on a marked spot and confirm the position reads what it should.

06

Local map

jobHold one consistent picture of the nearby floor, over time
incorridors — from 03detections — from 04odometry increment and absolute pose — from 05surveyed features — from 07 prior map
outthe nearby world in car-frame cm → 07, 08, 10

This is where the two sensor chains finally meet, and it's the last block before anything starts making decisions.

Anchoring memory to the floor

Remembering "there was a white line at row 180 of the picture" is almost useless. A pixel isn't a place. Next frame the car has moved, the picture has scrolled, and row 180 shows something else entirely. You'd be remembering something that has already stopped existing.

Remember a spot on the floor instead: there's a corner 22 cm ahead and 4 cm to my left. Then every frame, shift that memory by however far the car moved. 22 becomes 20, then 18, then 15. Eventually it slides under the bumper and out of sight, and you still know exactly where it is.

How corridors, detections and pose combine

The inputs arrive in different forms and play different roles, and the thing usually worth untangling is that only one of them moves the map.

  • Corridors and detections are observations. They already arrive in the current car frame, in centimetres, measured this frame. They get inserted, not transformed.
  • The odometry increment is the motion. It is the only thing that shifts what's already stored. It comes from wheels and gyro, so it is smooth and never jumps.
  • The absolute pose is a key, not a position. It never moves anything. It is used to look features up in the prior map, and to convert those features into the car frame.

One tick, in order:

1. MOVE    Δ = (dx, dy, dθ) since last tick, from 05's prediction
           for every stored item:
               p ← R(-dθ) · (p - [dx, dy])
           this is the only place anything moves

2. INSERT  new corridors and detections from 03 and 04
           already in the car frame — no transform needed

3. MATCH   for each new detection:
               find a stored item of the same type within ~10 cm
               found     -> fuse: pos ← (1-α)·stored + α·new
                                  conf ← raise
               not found -> add as new
           fusing beats replacing: each look is noisy, and
           averaging ten looks is worth about 3x in accuracy

4. PULL    if 07 says a surveyed feature is relevant here:
               T = inverse(absolute pose from 05)
               insert T · feature, tagged source = MAP
           this is the ONLY use of the absolute pose

5. AGE     drop items more than 150 cm behind the rear axle
           drop items unseen for more than 1.5 s
           decay confidence on anything not re-observed

Step 1 and step 4 are the two halves people usually blur together. Step 1 is dead reckoning: smooth, relative, accurate over short distances, drifting over long ones. Step 4 is a lookup: coarse, absolute, and only ever used to fetch things you surveyed in advance.

So the pose's ±20 cm error never touches a camera-derived corridor. It only affects items tagged MAP, which is exactly the trade you want — map objects sit where the map thinks they are, camera objects sit where the camera saw them, and a map object is only allowed to become the corridor where the camera has nothing to offer.

Write the disagreement rule down now. When the map and the camera describe the same thing differently, the camera wins on geometry and the map wins on identity. The map may tell you this is roundabout exit 2. It may not tell you the edge is here when the camera can see the edge.

Why three challenges need this block

Parking: the slot leaves the camera's view long before the car is inside it, and the manoeuvre in 10 needs the slot pose for the whole reversing sequence.

The boom gate: you stop on a line you can no longer see, because it is under the bumper.

The traffic light: it sits above and behind you for the entire time you wait at it, so what you're waiting on is a remembered object being re-confirmed by glimpses, not a live detection.

Four blocks that turn the picture into a decision, and the decision into a curve.

07

Prior map and mission planner

jobOwn the route — which section, which exit, which parking slot
innearby world — from 06 local mappose and covariance — from 05the surveyed track — loaded from disk at startup
outroute state and active manoeuvre → 08 behavioursurveyed geometry on request → 03, 06

The prior map isn't really a pipeline stage at all — it's a lookup table you load once at startup. At runtime it takes nothing in. You hand it a position and it tells you what is there.

Segment    { id, polyline[] in venue cm, width, type, next[] }
             type: STRAIGHT | CORNER | TUNNEL | ROUNDABOUT | PARK_BAY
Roundabout { centre, r_inner, r_outer, exits[{heading, segment}] }
Slot       { pose, length, width, kind: PARALLEL | PERPENDICULAR }
Landmark   { type, pos in venue cm }

The lookup is a projection. Given a pose, project it onto candidate polylines and take the nearest — but only test the current segment and its neighbours, never the whole map. That's cheaper, and more importantly it stops a bad pose from teleporting you to a segment on the far side of the track.

candidates = [current] + current.next + current.prev
best       = argmin over candidates of perpendicular distance
advance when the projection passes the segment's end by > 10 cm

The mission planner is the slow layer sitting on top of that. It's your satnav: it holds the route as an ordered list of segment ids, tracks how far along the current one you are, and decides which exit off the roundabout and which slot to park in. It changes a handful of times per lap, not sixty times a second.

What it emits is small:

RouteState {
  segment        id and type
  dist_to_end    cm
  next_event     GATE | LIGHT | BUMP | ROUNDABOUT | PARK | none
  dist_to_event  cm
  exit           which roundabout exit, when relevant
  slot           which parking slot, when relevant
  confident      false when 05's covariance is too large
}

What it deliberately doesn't do is steer or set a speed. Those belong to the block below, and keeping the route decision out of the frame-by-frame loop is what stops one bad frame from rerouting the whole run.

The confident flag is the escape hatch for the tunnel. When the pose covariance is large the route state is a guess, and 08 should fall back to plain corridor following rather than act on a map prediction it can't trust.

Check the pre-programming rule. Carrying a surveyed map is probably fine; a hardcoded list of turns probably isn't. Where the line sits decides how much this block is allowed to assume, so get it answered in writing before you build it. See 14.

08

Behaviour

jobTurn "I'm in this situation" into a goal
innearby world — from 06 local maproute state — from 07 mission planner
outone goal struct → 09 corridor generator or 10 manoeuvre planner

Planning is really three jobs happening at very different speeds, and this block is the middle one — the place where a decision stops being a word and becomes geometry.

Lateral and longitudinal

When you drive, your hands and your feet solve two different problems and mostly don't consult each other.

So run them as two separate lists of states at the same time, both filling in one shared instruction. This is the answer to the problem raised right at the start: if you only allow one state at a time, you can't say "I'm in the roundabout and stopped at a red light" — and on your track that can happen.

Priority lists, not transition graphs

Don't build a graph of "from state A you may go to B or C". With ten states you get ninety possible transitions, you'll implement forty of them, and the run will find one of the fifty you forgot.

Build each machine as an ordered list of guards instead. Every tick, walk the list from the top and take the first one whose condition is true. Any state can follow any state, nothing can deadlock, and adding a behaviour means inserting one line at the right priority.

lateral, highest priority first:
    PARK_MANOEUVRE     if route.next_event == PARK and dist < 60
    ROUNDABOUT_EXIT_N  if segment.type == ROUNDABOUT
    TRIM_FOR_OBSTACLE  if an obstacle overlaps the corridor within 80 cm
    FOLLOW_CORRIDOR    always — the fallback

longitudinal, highest priority first:
    STOP_AND_HOLD      if light == RED or gate == DOWN
                       or corridor.valid == false
    CREEP              if lateral == PARK_MANOEUVRE
    SLOW               if bump / hill / tunnel ahead, or curvature high
    CRUISE             always — the fallback

Notice that the failure behaviour falls out for free: if 03 publishes an invalid corridor, the longitudinal list matches STOP_AND_HOLD at the top and the car stops. You didn't write an error handler, you wrote a guard.

Trimming around an obstacle

This is the one state that reshapes the corridor rather than just selecting it, so it's worth spelling out. The obstacle is a footprint in the car frame from 06; the corridor is two lists of points.

for each slice s within the obstacle's span:
    edge = the obstacle's nearest side, in y
    if the obstacle sits right of centre:
        right[s] = min(right[s], edge - half_car - clearance)
    else:
        left[s]  = max(left[s],  edge + half_car + clearance)

    if right[s] - left[s] < car_width + margin:
        give up trimming — report "blocked" and let the
        longitudinal machine stop the car instead

The car never needs to know it is "changing lane". It's the same corridor with a bite taken out of it, and 09 does what it always does: find the best path inside the shape it was given.

The goal struct

This is the only thing this block passes down, and it looks the same whichever state you're in. Each state just fills the blanks differently. Nine challenges, one shape of instruction.

Goal {
  corridor       left[] and right[] bounds
  generator      CORRIDOR | MANOEUVRE
  target_speed   cm/s          <- longitudinal
  speed_cap      cm/s, ceiling <- longitudinal
  lookahead      cm            <- longitudinal
  goal_pose      x, y, heading <- lateral, MANOEUVRE only
}

The generator field is a switch: it picks which of the next two blocks gets to draw the curve.

Give every state a time limit. If the light detector fails and green never arrives, the car will sit there until the competition clock runs out. After so many seconds, force it back to normal driving.

Change the corridor gradually, not instantly. Snapping it 15 cm sideways in one frame asks for a steering jump the car can't make. Ramp the trim in over about 30 cm of arclength with a smoothstep, so the shape changes at a rate the car can follow.

Put hysteresis on every threshold. "Obstacle within 80 cm" flickers when the obstacle is at 80 cm. Enter at 80, leave at 100.

09

Trajectory generation

jobChoose an actual curve inside the region behaviour allowed
ingoal struct — from 08 behaviour, when generator = CORRIDORlast frame's chosen path — from itself
outtrajectory: ordered poses with target speeds → 11 controller

Behaviour said where you may go. This block says where you will go. The gap between those two is the whole reason it exists.

The day-one version

Build this first and don't apologise for it. It's twenty lines and it will drive the car round most of the track.

resample left[] and right[] at equal arclength
centre[i] = midpoint(left[i], right[i])
fit  y = a + b·x + c·x²   by least squares over the next 60 cm
speed at each point = min(goal.target_speed,
                          sqrt(a_lat_max / |curvature|))

The curvature speed cap is the part people skip and then wonder why the car lifts a wheel at the roundabout. A path of curvature κ driven at speed v pulls v²κ sideways; cap v so that stays inside what your tyres and centre of gravity tolerate.

How the sampler works

The upgrade is less clever than it sounds. There's no formula for the perfect path, so you don't look for one. You guess a lot of paths and keep the least bad.

The key idea is to stop working in x and y and work in the corridor's own coordinates instead: s along the corridor centreline, d sideways off it. In those coordinates "stay inside the corridor" stops being a geometry problem and becomes a pair of inequalities on one number.

current state -> (s0, d0, d0')      # offset and heading error right now

for s_f in {40, 60, 80} cm:         # how far ahead to finish
  for d_f in -12..+12 cm step 3:    # where to finish, sideways
      fit a cubic d(s) with
          d(0)   = d0,  d'(0)   = d0'   # continuous with the car
          d(s_f) = d_f, d'(s_f) = 0     # arrive parallel to the corridor
      convert (s,d) back to x,y along the corridor

Pinning d(0) and d'(0) to the car's current offset and heading is what makes every candidate drivable. A path that starts sideways to your nose is no use to anyone, and this constraint makes those impossible to generate rather than something you filter out later.

Filter, then score

Filtering is yes or no, with no scoring. Most guesses die here — that's what they're for.

reject if  max |curvature| > 1 / R_min      # R_min = L / tan(δ_max)
reject if  any point leaves [left + half_car, right - half_car]
reject if  the car footprint along it hits an obstacle box
reject if  the lateral shift implies a rate the steering can't make

That first test is the first time in the whole pipeline that the car's own geometry enters the picture. R_min comes from the wheelbase and the maximum steering angle, and it's why a turn has to begin before the corner rather than at it — the point Appendix A makes about the old code.

Then give each survivor a score:

cost =  w1 · Σ  proximity to the corridor edge
      + w2 · Σ  curvature²
      + w3 ·    (d_f - d_centre)²
      + w4 · Σ  |d(s) - d_previous(s)|        # disagreement with last frame
pick the minimum

That last term matters more than it looks. When two paths score almost the same, the planner keeps flipping between them and your steering shakes. Charging a small penalty for changing its mind settles it down. Make all four weights adjustable while the car is running — you'll spend real time on them.

Which version to build first

This isn't about taking the easy route. It's about being able to find bugs. If the simple version already drives, and you swap in the sampler and things break, you know what broke — you changed one box. If you start with the sampler and the car misbehaves, you have no idea whether it's the sampler, the cameras, the corridors or the map, because nothing has ever worked.

This is the payoff from the interface rule in 01, and it's worth noticing how cheap it was to get.

10

Manoeuvres

jobGet the car into a parking slot, reversing included
ingoal struct with goal_pose — from 08, when generator = MANOEUVREremembered slot pose — from 06 local map
outtrajectory with explicit direction changes → 11 controller

Parking is the one job the corridor sampler can't do, so it gets its own generator sitting in the same slot.

Three things make it a different problem. The sampler only ever goes forward; parallel parking needs reverse. The sampler only ever stays inside a lane; parking has to leave one. And the sampler is happy ending up anywhere sensible, while parking has exactly one acceptable final position and angle.

Solve it, don't search it

The textbook answer is a general planner — hybrid A* or Reeds–Shepp curves over a grid. It's the right tool when you don't know the slot shape in advance. You do know it, so it's overkill, and it is miserable to debug at three in the morning.

Parallel parking with car-like steering has a closed-form two-arc solution. Turn full lock one way, then full lock the other, and the two arcs are mirror images. From the slot dimensions and the car's R_min you solve directly for where the manoeuvre has to start.

R = R_min + safety

given the slot and the car's required final pose:
    the two arc centres lie a distance 2R apart
    solve for the switch point where the arcs meet
    start pose = final pose, walked back along both arcs

plan = [ leg(FORWARD, straight to the start pose),
         leg(REVERSE, arc at -δ_max),
         leg(REVERSE, arc at +δ_max),
         leg(FORWARD, short straighten) ]

Perpendicular parking is simpler still — one arc plus a straight, often drivable nose-first. Build the reversing case first anyway: if reverse works, forward is free.

Legs and cusps

The output isn't one smooth curve. It's a list of legs, each with a direction, and the joints between them are cusps where the car must actually come to a stop before reversing. Hand that structure to 11 explicitly rather than hoping it works it out from the geometry.

When to replan

The surface is slippery and the wheels lie to you, so the car will drift off the plan.

replan if  cross-track error to the current leg > 4 cm
replan if  the slot pose in the local map has moved > 5 cm
           since the plan was made
otherwise  keep the existing plan

This is the opposite of 09, which replans from scratch every single frame. The difference is that a corridor path is a suggestion for the next half second, while a parking plan is a commitment several seconds long — and re-deciding a commitment every frame is how you get a car that shuffles back and forth and never finishes.

Control

Two blocks that turn a curve into servo angles, and one last thing that can overrule everyone.

11

Trajectory controller

jobDrive whatever path it was handed
intrajectory — from 09 or 10, identical format either waymeasured speed — from the wheel encoders
outsteering angle and target speed → 12 safety reflex

One controller drives any path you give it, whether it came from lane following or from parking. It has no idea which. The method is called pure pursuit: pick a point on the path a little way ahead, then work out the steering angle that curves you onto it.

δ  = atan( 2 · L · sin(α) / Ld )

L  = wheelbase
α  = angle from the car's heading to the lookahead point
Ld = lookahead distance

Finding that point is the part the formula doesn't show:

i = index of the trajectory point closest to the rear axle
walk forward from i, accumulating arclength, until ≥ Ld
interpolate between the last two points -> target
α = atan2(target.y, target.x)          # already in the car frame

if the current leg is REVERSE: walk backward, and flip sign(δ)
if a cusp lies before the lookahead: clamp target speed to zero

Because everything is already in the car frame, α is a single atan2 with no transform. That's the quiet dividend of 02 putting the whole pipeline into centimetres from the rear axle.

The lookahead distance

How far ahead you aim should grow with speed. But the limits matter more than the slope:

Ld = clamp( k · v , Ld_min , Ld_max )

Ld_min can't be zero, or the maths divides by zero the moment the car stops. Set it to about your wheelbase. Ld_max stops the car aiming so far ahead on a straight that it starts cutting the next corner before it gets there. Start k at roughly half a second of travel.

That single number is the biggest lever on how the car feels. Too small and it weaves; too large and it corners lazily and wide. Make it live-tunable on day one.

The speed side

Pure pursuit only gives you steering. Speed is a separate, simpler loop: take the target speed off the trajectory, measure the actual speed from the encoders, close a PI loop onto a motor command. Feed-forward the nominal command for that speed so the integrator only has to correct for the hill rather than do all the work.

12

Safety reflex and motor output

jobLast chance to stop, then talk to the hardware
insteering and speed — from 11 controllerdepth and detection states — from 02 and 04, directly
outservo pulse width and motor PWM → the hardware

The behaviour block handles the stops it saw coming — red light, gate down. Underneath it sits one final check that can force the speed to zero no matter what the planner asked for, for when something goes wrong faster than the state machine can react.

Note that it reads its inputs straight from perception rather than through planning. That's the point: it's a short circuit around the part of the system most likely to be wrong.

speed = command.speed
if a non-floor return is closer than 15 cm inside the corridor: speed = 0
if the corridor has been invalid for more than 5 frames:        speed = 0
if no perception update for more than 200 ms:                   speed = 0
if gate == DOWN and distance to the gate < 10 cm:               speed = 0

steering = command.steering      # never modified, ever

It can stop the car. It can't steer it. That restriction is the point: a reflex that's allowed to steer is a second, hidden planner, and the two will eventually disagree about which way to swerve.

Motor output

Two things to get right in the last block. Rate-limit the steering so the servo is never asked to snap instantly. And don't assume steering angle maps linearly onto servo pulse width — on a cheap Ackermann linkage it doesn't, and the error is worst at large angles, which is exactly where parking and the roundabout live.

δ_cmd = clamp(δ, δ_prev - rate·dt, δ_prev + rate·dt)
pulse = interpolate(calibration_table, δ_cmd)

# building the table: jack the car up, command a pulse width,
# measure the actual front wheel angle with a protractor.
# nine points, full left to full right. once, on paper.

Measure that curve once and put the table here, where it belongs, rather than letting every block above quietly compensate for it.

13

Block reference

The whole pipeline in order, for when you want to check an interface without reading a section. Get these right and any one block can be rewritten without touching the ones around it.

02Capture and warp

Undistorts three camera frames and projects each through its own homography into one shared top-down image in centimetres from the rear axle. Also computes the ground-plane mask from depth.

in: three raw frames, depth  ·  out: ground image + floor mask → 03; raw frame + clusters → 04

03Corridors

Grows the road region from a seed under the bumper, finds divider lines sitting inside it, slices it into a left and right edge. At the roundabout the shape comes from the surveyed map instead, and nothing after it can tell the difference.

in: ground image + mask ← 02; map geometry ← 07  ·  out: corridors → 06

04Landmarks

Reads the raw frame and depth for the discrete things the car reacts to: traffic light by lamp rank, gate by depth band, signs by template, obstacles from the non-floor mask. Debounced, reported as floor positions.

in: raw frame, depth, clusters ← 02  ·  out: detections → 06; light and gate state → 12

05EKF localisation

Fuses UWB, gyro rate and wheel speed into one venue-frame pose while estimating gyro bias, with outlier gating on UWB. Publishes both a jumpy absolute pose and a smooth relative increment. Never used for steering.

in: UWB, gyro, encoders  ·  out: pose + covariance → 06, 07; odometry → 06

06Local map

Moves everything stored by the odometry increment, inserts and fuses new observations, pulls surveyed features in using the absolute pose, ages out what's behind. The only place the two sensor chains meet.

in: corridors ← 03; detections ← 04; pose ← 05; features ← 07  ·  out: nearby world → 07, 08, 10

07Prior map

The surveyed track in venue coordinates: segment polylines, roundabout geometry and exits, parking slot poses, landmark locations. Static, loaded at startup. Not a pipeline stage — a lookup table.

in: nothing at runtime  ·  out: what exists at a given pose → 03, 06, 07

07Mission planner

Owns the route. Projects the pose onto the current segment and its neighbours, advances with hysteresis, reports the next event and its distance. Flags itself unconfident when the pose covariance grows.

in: nearby world ← 06; pose ← 05; prior map  ·  out: route state → 08

08Behaviour — lateral

A priority list, first match wins: park manoeuvre, roundabout exit, trim for obstacle, follow corridor. Trimming reshapes the corridor rather than switching mode.

in: nearby world ← 06; route state ← 07  ·  out: corridor bounds, generator choice, goal pose

08Behaviour — longitudinal

A second priority list running concurrently and never consulting the first: stop and hold, creep, slow, cruise. An invalid corridor matches the top guard, so failure stops the car without an error handler.

in: nearby world ← 06; route state ← 07  ·  out: target speed, speed cap, lookahead

09Corridor generator

Turns a corridor into a trajectory. Day one: average the two bounds, fit a quadratic, cap speed by curvature. Later: sample lateral offsets in the corridor's own frame, filter the infeasible, score the rest. Replans every frame.

in: goal struct ← 08  ·  out: poses with target speeds → 11

10Manoeuvre planner

Closed-form two-arc parking solution, emitted as legs with explicit direction changes and cusps. Replans only on drift, not every frame.

in: goal pose ← 08; slot pose ← 06  ·  out: poses with target speeds → 11

11Trajectory controller

Pure pursuit with a speed-scaled lookahead, forward and reverse variants, plus a PI speed loop on the encoders. Tracks any trajectory regardless of which generator produced it.

in: trajectory ← 09 or 10; encoder speed  ·  out: steering angle, target speed → 12

12Safety reflex

Reads depth and detections directly, bypassing planning. Can force speed to zero; cannot touch steering. Also the watchdog on a stalled perception thread.

in: command ← 11; depth ← 02; detections ← 04  ·  out: final command

12Motor output

Slew-limits the steering, then converts angle to servo pulse width through a measured calibration table. Speed to PWM with feed-forward plus the PI correction.

in: final command  ·  out: hardware

14

Build order and open questions

The debug renderer

Before anything else on this list: a live top-down picture of what the car thinks is around it. Corridor edges, landmarks, the path it picked, where it thinks it is, and the candidate paths once the sampler exists. It takes an afternoon and it's the most useful thing in this whole document.

It's how you tell "the camera got it wrong" from "the planner got it wrong" in thirty seconds instead of a whole evening. Most teams skip it. Don't.

Order of work

  • Raise and tilt the camera. Lock exposure and white balance. Free, and changes more than any algorithm here.
  • Raise the 15 px per frame shift clamp in the existing detector. One line; may fix the corners outright while you build the real thing.
  • Build the renderer.
  • Calibrate intrinsics, then the homographies, from points you measured on the floor rather than guessed ratios.
  • Add the depth ground-plane mask.
  • Build the corridors block — region growing, slicing, divider detection. This is what makes the two-lane section and the roundabout possible at all.
  • Centreline of the corridor, plus pure pursuit. The car drives.
  • Build the local map on the floor, then add the EKF and survey the track.
  • Mission planner and the two behaviour machines.
  • Manoeuvre planner for parking.
  • Swap the centreline fit for the corridor sampler.

Notice the shape of that list: the car is driving at step seven, with three of the fourteen blocks written properly and the rest stubbed out. Everything after that is replacing a stupid block with a good one, one at a time.

Confirm with the organisers

  • Pre-programming. The rules say no pre-programming. Carrying a surveyed map of the track is probably fine. A hardcoded list of turns probably isn't. Get an answer in writing — it decides how much the mission planner is allowed to assume.
  • Transverse lines. Do marked lines cross the road at the 90° corners? If so they make excellent odometry-correcting triggers. If not, that idea disappears.
  • Parking sign placement. Does it face the approaching car, or sit at the side of the slot? The answer decides whether a side camera has to read it.
  • Venue. Listed as subject to change. UWB anchors need setup and survey time on site — budget it into the Day 1 open-track sessions and make the mapping process repeatable in an hour.

Known risks

  • The tunnel, where UWB and the camera are both at their worst simultaneously.
  • The car sitting lower or higher on the hill and the bump, which quietly breaks both the homography and the ground-plane test.
  • Two maps that can disagree. Decide the rule now and write it down: the surveyed map wins on what's coming next, the live camera wins on where you are in the lane. Never let the map overrule what the car can see right now.
A

Why the current line follower breaks

This is the code the architecture replaces. It's at the end rather than the start because it's the motivation, not a part of the design — but it's worth reading once, because both failures show up again as blocks above.

Row scanning

Right now the code looks across the picture one row at a time. It finds white on the left, white on the right, and steers toward the middle. That works on a straight road. It only works on a straight road.

In a tight corner the road swings sideways and slides out of the picture. Now a row across the image might catch only one line, or catch the same line twice. The code averages whatever it found anyway, so it ends up steering from two things that aren't the lane at all.

The shift clamp

There's a line in the detector that limits how fast the search window is allowed to move. It can shift 15 pixels per frame, on a picture 320 pixels wide. In a tight corner the road slides across the picture much faster than that. The window falls behind, loses the line, and the filter starts guessing from old numbers.

So the corner problem isn't really about detecting white. It's a setting that makes the tracker slow on purpose, which is the last thing you want at the roundabout. Raising that number is a one-line change. Try it first — it may buy you a working car while the real pipeline gets built.

No model of the car

The code also knows nothing about the car it's driving. Your car can't spin on the spot. There's a smallest circle it can drive, and that circle decides where a turn has to start. The answer is always before the corner, never at it.
